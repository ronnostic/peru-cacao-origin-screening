The business question

Can an organization show where its cacao comes from, and what needs a closer look?

Investor readiness is more than a map. It includes traceable origin, reliable records, operating capacity, volumes, contracts, cash flow, and a clear account of unresolved risks. This demo tests one small but useful part of that picture: whether public spatial data can help a team decide where to verify first.

What did we do?

We compared mapped cacao plot polygons [1] with mapped forest-loss polygons for 2019–2022 [2], then summarized the overlap by plot ID.

The outcome

A useful signal, with a very clear boundary.

The GIS screen helps prioritize questions. It does not decide whether a product is compliant, financeable, or safe to buy.

What does it mean?

Put the records in review order.

Start by reconciling the highest-overlap plot IDs against source imagery, parcel history, producer records, and the actual supply chain. Then investigate unmatched records separately.

No match in the join means “not matched.” It does not mean “no forest loss.”

Who cares about it?

People who need evidence before a decision.

  • Producers and cooperativesSee which plot records need stronger location or history evidence.
  • Exporters and buyersFocus traceability follow-up before sourcing or onboarding.
  • Investors and lendersSeparate a fixable information gap from a risk that needs specialist review.
  • Technical and compliance teamsKeep geospatial flags tied to sources, dates, and review status.

The ranking is a triage aid, not an automated pass/fail score. A red polygon can start a conversation. It cannot finish one.

How the analysis was built

A transparent stack, with the boring parts left in.

The repairs, coordinate systems, filters, joins, and missing matches are part of the result. Hiding them would make the map prettier and the decision worse.

01

Load and inspect

Loaded the cacao GeoJSON [1] and the SERFOR/MINAM ArcGIS forest-loss layer [2]. Set the working view to the study extent.

02

Check and repair geometry

The cacao file had 461 valid polygons and one self-intersection. Fix Geometries produced 462 valid polygons. The clipped forest-loss layer was also repaired and then passed a validity check with 41,717 valid features and no reported errors.

03

Filter and overlay

Filtered the forest-loss layer [2] to Año = 2019–2022, clipped the layer to the cacao study extent, then intersected it with the plot polygons.

04

Summarize by plot

Grouped overlap features by PLOT_ID, joined the summary back to the 462-plot layer, and calculated the share of plot area that overlaps mapped forest-loss polygons.

05

Style for review

Applied a graduated color ramp to sort the plot-level screen. In the ranked-table capture, the yellow polygon is QGIS’s selected-feature highlight. The status bar says “Selected: 1.” It is not a separate risk class.

The calculations

Plot area in hectares

area($geometry) / 10000

Share overlapping mapped loss

if("farm_ha" > 0,
100 * coalesce("loss_sum", 0) / "farm_ha",
NULL)

The expression converts a missing join value to zero. That can make an unmatched plot look like zero overlap. The next version should carry an explicit match-status field.

From map to decision

Turn a polygon flag into a useful next step.

A real diligence workflow would connect each spatial observation to the documents and people who can explain it. The goal is a better review queue, not an automatic rejection button.

First

Reconcile identity

Confirm that each PLOT_ID points to the right producer and current production boundary.

Then

Check the timeline

Review dated imagery and land-use history around the relevant date. Annual 2020 data cannot locate change relative to 31 December 2020.

Connect

Trace the shipment

Link verified plots to purchase records, harvest periods, cooperative lots, and export batches.

Decide

Assign a human review

Record what evidence supports the claim, what remains unresolved, who owns the follow-up, and when to revisit it.

Where RAG or Jev could fit later

They were not used in this GIS demo. In a partner-backed workflow, retrieval could bring the dated buyer requirement and supporting documents into one evidence bundle. Jev could answer a bounded question about that bundle, while ordinary code handles exact fields, arithmetic, and routing. A reviewer would still approve the action and see the source records.

Read this before using the numbers

A screen is a starting point, not a verdict.

Time windows do not line up with a compliance test

The forest-loss screen uses annual observations for 2019–2022. The EUDR deforestation cutoff is 31 December 2020. The selected period includes pre-cutoff years, and annual data cannot pinpoint a change to that date. The cacao dataset description says its polygons were interpreted from satellite imagery beginning in 2020. It does not establish complete coverage through 2024. Read the regulation.

Missing join rows are unknown, not clean

377 plot IDs did not match an overlap-summary row. Because the field calculator uses coalesce("loss_sum", 0), some may display as zero. Treat those records as “no summary match” until the join and source coverage are checked.

The polygons are not a supplier register

This demonstration used public mapped cacao areas. It did not use producer identities, plot ownership or use rights, purchases, volumes, batch links, field visits, or a buyer's requirements. It cannot establish that a specific organization supplied a specific shipment.

Overlap does not establish cause or legal status

A mapped intersection can reflect timing, classification, geometry, or source-data issues. A qualified reviewer must examine imagery, land history, and applicable legal definitions before drawing a compliance conclusion.
